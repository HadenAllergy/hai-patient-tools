COVID-19 and Urticaria

When a Virus Teaches Your Mast Cells Bad Habits

The Short Version of a Long Story

Since 2020, allergists have noticed something that was not in any pre-pandemic textbook: a substantial number of patients developing chronic hives for the first time either during or shortly after a COVID-19 infection. Not a handful. Not a statistical oddity. Hundreds of patients across clinical practices, enough to make "When did the hives start?" and "Did you have COVID around that time?" standard intake questions in allergy offices nationwide.

This handout explains the connection between SARS-CoV-2 infection and urticaria — what we know with confidence, what we strongly suspect but cannot yet prove, and what it means for your treatment. We will also address the separate (and much smaller) question of whether COVID-19 vaccination can trigger or worsen hives, because it comes up in nearly every conversation on this topic.

COVID-19 and New-Onset Urticaria: The Clinical Picture

The pattern is consistent enough to describe, even if the precise mechanism is still being mapped. A patient contracts COVID-19 — sometimes severe, sometimes mild, sometimes barely symptomatic. During the acute infection or within the weeks that follow, hives appear. Sometimes the urticaria is acute and self-limited, resolving as the infection clears. But in a meaningful subset of patients, the hives persist well beyond the infection — weeks, months, and in some cases a year or more — meeting the clinical definition of chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU): daily or near-daily wheals lasting longer than six weeks without an identifiable external trigger.

The urticaria can present as classic wheals (raised, itchy, migratory, blanching), angioedema (deeper swelling of the lips, eyelids, hands, or feet), or both. It behaves, clinically, like any other chronic spontaneous urticaria — because that is what it is. The COVID-19 infection did not create a new disease. It activated a disease process that the immune system is quite capable of running on its own once started.

The timing is the diagnostic clue. When a patient with no prior history of urticaria develops persistent hives within days to weeks of a confirmed or suspected COVID-19 infection, the temporal association is strong enough to be clinically meaningful — even when we cannot yet draw a straight mechanistic line from the virus to the wheal.

How COVID-19 Triggers Hives: The Mechanisms

The relationship between SARS-CoV-2 and urticaria is not a single pathway. It is a convergence of several mechanisms, any combination of which can push the immune system from quiet tolerance into mast cell chaos.

Direct Mast Cell Activation

SARS-CoV-2 can activate mast cells directly, independent of IgE. The virus interacts with toll-like receptors and other pattern-recognition receptors on mast cell surfaces, triggering degranulation and the release of histamine, prostaglandins, leukotrienes, and pro-inflammatory cytokines. This is not a traditional allergic reaction — the virus is not an allergen — but the downstream effect is identical: wheals, itch, angioedema. The mast cell does not care whether the signal came from an IgE antibody recognizing peanut protein or from a viral particle binding a pattern-recognition receptor. It degranulates either way.

The Cytokine Storm and Its Aftermath

COVID-19, particularly in moderate-to-severe cases, triggers a systemic inflammatory response characterized by elevated interleukin-1, interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, and other pro-inflammatory cytokines. This cytokine surge lowers the threshold for mast cell activation throughout the body — mast cells that were previously sitting quietly in the skin become primed and hyperreactive. In some patients, this hyperreactivity resolves as the cytokine levels normalize. In others, the mast cells appear to settle into a new, lower activation threshold that persists long after the virus is cleared.

Think of it as a thermostat that has been bumped. The virus cranked the immune system's inflammatory thermostat up during the acute infection, and in some patients, it never quite resets to its pre-infection setting.

Autoimmune Triggering

This is where it gets particularly interesting for allergists. A significant proportion of chronic spontaneous urticaria — estimated at 30 to 50 percent of cases — is driven by autoantibodies: IgG antibodies directed against IgE itself or against the high-affinity IgE receptor (FcεRI) on mast cells. These autoantibodies cross-link the receptors and trigger degranulation in the absence of any external allergen. This is autoimmune urticaria — the immune system attacking its own mast cell machinery.

Viral infections are well-established triggers of autoantibody production through molecular mimicry (viral proteins resembling self-proteins), bystander activation (immune stimulation spilling over to self-reactive lymphocytes), and epitope spreading (tissue damage exposing previously hidden self-antigens to the immune system). SARS-CoV-2 has been associated with the development of multiple autoantibodies — antiphospholipid antibodies, anti-nuclear antibodies, and others — across a range of organ systems. The production of anti-IgE or anti-FcεRI autoantibodies as a consequence of COVID-19 infection is biologically plausible and consistent with the clinical observation of persistent post-COVID urticaria that behaves identically to autoimmune CSU.

In plain terms: COVID-19 may, in some patients, trick the immune system into producing antibodies that attack its own mast cell receptors — permanently arming the mast cells to fire without any external trigger. The virus leaves, but the autoimmune process it started does not.

The Th2 Shift

Post-COVID immune dysregulation can shift the balance of T-helper cell populations toward a Th2-dominant state — the same immune skewing that drives allergic disease in general. In predisposed individuals (those with a personal or family history of atopy), this shift may lower the threshold for IgE-mediated sensitization and mast cell reactivity, making the emergence of urticaria more likely. This is not COVID-19 causing an allergy to something specific. It is the virus recalibrating the immune system's overall tendency toward allergic-type responses.

COVID-19 as an Unmasking Agent

There is a concept in immunology that deserves its own section because it reframes how patients and clinicians should think about post-COVID urticaria: unmasking.

Some patients who develop hives after COVID-19 were not immunologically normal before the infection. They were, to borrow a geological metaphor, sitting on a fault line — a genetically predisposed, subclinically primed immune system that had not yet produced symptoms. Perhaps their mast cells were slightly more reactive than average. Perhaps they had low-level autoantibodies that had not yet reached a clinically significant titer. Perhaps their Th2 skewing was present but compensated.

Then COVID-19 arrived — a systemic immune provocation of enormous magnitude — and pushed them over the threshold. The virus did not create the disease from nothing. It revealed a disease that was waiting for a trigger. This is the same phenomenon allergists observe with other major immune provocations: surgery, severe stress, pregnancy, or other viral infections can all unmask latent urticaria in predisposed individuals.

What this means for you: If you developed hives for the first time after COVID-19, the virus was likely the trigger, not the cause. The distinction matters because your treatment targets the urticaria itself — the mast cell dysfunction, the possible autoantibodies, the Th2 inflammation — not the virus. COVID-19 may have opened the door, but the immune process that walked through it is the one your allergist needs to manage.

Worsening of Pre-Existing Urticaria After COVID-19

Patients who already have chronic spontaneous urticaria and then contract COVID-19 frequently experience flares — sometimes dramatic ones. A maintenance dose of antihistamines that had been controlling symptoms for months suddenly becomes inadequate. Wheals become larger, more frequent, or more widespread. Angioedema episodes may appear for the first time in patients who previously had only wheals.

The mechanisms are the same ones described above — cytokine-mediated mast cell priming, potential new autoantibody production, and Th2 immune skewing — but applied to a system that was already dysregulated. COVID-19 in a patient with pre-existing CSU is like throwing gasoline on a campfire that was already burning. The fire does not change its fundamental nature, but it burns hotter and wider.

The clinical approach is to escalate treatment as needed during and after the infection — stepping up antihistamine dosing (up to four times the standard dose per international guidelines), adding H2 blockers or leukotriene antagonists, and in refractory cases, moving to omalizumab or other advanced therapies. Most patients return to their pre-COVID baseline over weeks to months, though some require a permanently higher level of treatment.

COVID-19 Vaccination and Urticaria

This question comes up constantly, and the answer is reassuring.

COVID-19 vaccines — particularly the mRNA vaccines — can trigger transient urticaria in a small percentage of recipients. The best available data suggest approximately 1 to 2 percent of vaccine recipients experience some form of urticarial reaction, with the rate modestly higher (approximately 3 to 5 percent) in patients with pre-existing chronic urticaria. These reactions are overwhelmingly self-limited, resolving within days to weeks, and respond to standard antihistamine therapy.

In patients with pre-existing CSU, vaccination may trigger a flare of baseline symptoms — a temporary uptick in wheal frequency or severity — that typically returns to pre-vaccination baseline within one to two weeks. Pre-treatment with antihistamines on the day of vaccination can minimize this.

Anaphylaxis to COVID-19 vaccines is exceedingly rare — approximately 2 to 5 cases per million doses. Pre-existing chronic urticaria, food allergy, environmental allergy, and drug allergy do not increase the risk of vaccine anaphylaxis. Patients with a history of urticaria can be vaccinated safely with standard post-vaccination observation periods.

The risk calculation is straightforward: A COVID-19 infection carries a meaningful risk of triggering new-onset chronic urticaria, worsening existing urticaria, and producing lasting immune dysregulation. A COVID-19 vaccine carries a small risk of transient urticaria lasting days. The vaccine protects against the infection. For patients with urticaria, vaccination is not just safe — it is protective.

What Your Allergist Does About Post-COVID Urticaria

The good news is that post-COVID urticaria, whether new-onset or flared, is treated the same way as any other chronic spontaneous urticaria. The virus may have been the trigger, but the disease it activated responds to the same evidence-based treatment algorithm.

Step 1: Second-generation antihistamines (cetirizine, levocetirizine, fexofenadine, loratadine, desloratadine) at standard dose, escalated up to four times the standard dose per international urticaria guidelines. This is the foundation. Most patients achieve meaningful control at updosed antihistamines.
Step 2: Add-on agents for incomplete antihistamine response — H2 receptor antagonists (famotidine) and leukotriene receptor antagonists (montelukast). These address histamine and leukotriene pathways that H1 antihistamines alone do not fully cover.
Step 3: Omalizumab (Xolair) for patients who fail updosed antihistamines. Omalizumab binds free IgE and downregulates FcεRI expression on mast cells, directly addressing both IgE-mediated and autoimmune mechanisms. It is particularly effective in post-infectious urticaria where autoantibody production may be driving the disease.
Step 4: Immunosuppressive or immunomodulatory therapy (cyclosporine, most commonly) for the rare patient who fails omalizumab. This level of treatment is uncommon but available.

Your allergist will also evaluate for other post-COVID complications that can mimic or coexist with urticaria — thyroid dysfunction (autoimmune thyroiditis is another post-COVID autoimmune phenomenon and is independently associated with CSU), mast cell activation syndrome, and drug reactions from medications started during the COVID illness.

The Natural History: How Long Will This Last?

If you developed urticaria after COVID-19 and you are wondering whether it will be permanent, the honest answer is: probably not, but possibly.

The natural history of post-infectious chronic urticaria — whether triggered by COVID-19 or by other viral infections — follows a pattern of gradual improvement. Published data on CSU in general suggest that approximately 50 percent of patients achieve remission within one to three years, and roughly 80 percent within five years. Post-COVID urticaria appears to follow a similar trajectory, though the condition is too recent for long-term cohort data to be definitive.

Patients with evidence of autoimmune urticaria (positive autologous serum skin test or positive basophil activation test) tend to have a longer course than those without autoimmune markers. Patients who respond well to omalizumab sometimes achieve durable remission after a period of treatment — the drug does not just suppress symptoms but may, by reducing IgE and FcεRI expression, interrupt the autoimmune cycle that sustains the disease.

The trajectory is generally toward improvement. The timeline is unpredictable. And the treatment works while you wait.

The Bottom Line: COVID-19 infection can trigger new-onset chronic urticaria, worsen pre-existing hives, and activate autoimmune pathways that sustain mast cell dysfunction long after the virus is cleared. The mechanisms include direct mast cell activation, cytokine-mediated priming, autoantibody production, and Th2 immune skewing. COVID-19 vaccination, by contrast, carries only a small risk of transient urticaria and is protective against the much larger immune disruption of actual infection. Post-COVID urticaria is treated with the same evidence-based algorithm as any chronic spontaneous urticaria — antihistamines, omalizumab, and time. If you developed hives during or after COVID, tell your allergist. The connection matters for your care.
When to Seek Emergency Care

EMERGENCY: Call 911 or go to the emergency room if you experience difficulty breathing, throat tightness or swelling, dizziness or fainting, rapid heartbeat, or widespread swelling (lips, tongue, throat) during a hives episode — whether related to COVID-19 infection, vaccination, or any other cause. Anaphylaxis is a medical emergency that requires immediate treatment with epinephrine. If you have been prescribed an epinephrine auto-injector, use it and call 911. Do not wait to see if symptoms improve.
