Severe Drug Reactions: SJS, TEN, and DRESS

When Medications Turn Dangerous: Three Reactions You Need to Know

Haden Allergy & Immunology • Patient Education Series

Every medication comes with a leaflet full of potential side effects—most of them mild, most of them uncommon. But buried in the fine print of certain drugs are three reactions that are neither mild nor something to manage at home. Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS), toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), and drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS) are the rare, serious, and occasionally life-threatening reactions that make pharmacologists lose sleep. They are uncommon enough that most doctors will see only a handful in an entire career, but important enough that every patient on certain medications should know the warning signs.

This handout is not meant to scare you away from your prescriptions. It is meant to arm you with the knowledge to recognize trouble early—because with these reactions, early recognition is the single most important factor in outcome.

The Common Thread

All three reactions are severe hypersensitivity responses to medications. Your immune system, which normally protects you from infections, misidentifies a drug (or its breakdown product) as a threat and launches an attack against your own tissues. The target is different for each reaction—skin for SJS and TEN, multiple organs for DRESS—but the underlying principle is the same: your immune system has gone rogue.

Importantly, these are not dose-dependent reactions. You do not get SJS because you took too much medication. You get it because your immune system, often guided by your genetics, has a specific vulnerability to that particular drug. This is why pharmacogenomic testing (checking your genes before prescribing certain medications) is becoming increasingly standard.

Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS)

What it is: A severe skin reaction in which the outer layer of skin (epidermis) separates from the deeper layers. It involves less than 10% of the body surface area. Think of it as your skin blistering and peeling away in patches, often starting with painful red or purple spots that spread and merge.

What it looks like: It typically begins with flu-like symptoms—fever, malaise, sore throat—followed within days by a painful rash that develops blisters. The mucous membranes are almost always involved: mouth sores, eye redness and crusting, and genital lesions. The skin is tender to touch, and blisters can be rubbed off with gentle pressure (a positive Nikolsky sign).

Most common triggers: Sulfonamide antibiotics (sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim), anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, phenytoin, lamotrigine), allopurinol, and NSAIDs (particularly piroxicam and other oxicam-class drugs). Nevirapine is also a notable trigger.

Timeline: Usually appears 1–3 weeks after starting the offending drug.

Mortality: Approximately 1–5%. Most deaths result from sepsis or multi-organ failure.

Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN)

What it is: The severe end of the same spectrum as SJS. TEN involves detachment of more than 30% of the body surface area. Between 10–30% is called SJS-TEN overlap. TEN is essentially a full-body skin emergency.

What it looks like: Widespread sheets of skin detach, leaving raw, painful surfaces that resemble a severe burn. The patient looks and feels like a burn victim. Mucous membranes are heavily involved. Fluid loss, temperature instability, and infection risk are enormous.

Same triggers as SJS: The same medications, the same genetic vulnerabilities. TEN is not a different disease—it is the same disease at its most severe.

Mortality: 25–35%. TEN patients are typically treated in burn units or intensive care units.

DRESS Syndrome

What it is: Drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms. Unlike SJS/TEN, which primarily attacks the skin, DRESS goes after your internal organs—most commonly the liver, but also the kidneys, lungs, and heart.

What it looks like: A widespread, measles-like rash that may be itchy. Facial swelling is common and distinctive. Fever is almost always present. Lymph nodes may be enlarged. The real danger is the organ involvement you cannot see—liver enzymes climbing, kidney function declining, or inflammation of the heart muscle.

Most common triggers: Anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, lamotrigine), allopurinol, sulfonamide antibiotics, dapsone, and minocycline.

Timeline: Longer than SJS/TEN—2–6 weeks after starting the drug.

The viral connection: DRESS is frequently associated with reactivation of human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6). The drug appears to wake up a dormant virus, which amplifies the immune response.

Mortality: Approximately 5–10%, primarily from liver failure or myocarditis.

How They Differ at a Glance

  • SJS: Skin blistering and peeling (<10% body surface). Mucous membranes involved. 1–3 week onset. Mortality 1–5%.
  • TEN: Massive skin detachment (>30% body surface). Burn-unit care required. Same onset as SJS. Mortality 25–35%.
  • DRESS: Rash plus internal organ damage (liver, kidneys, heart). Facial swelling. 2–6 week onset. Eosinophilia. Viral reactivation. Mortality 5–10%.

Genetic Risk Factors

Certain HLA gene variants dramatically increase the risk of these reactions with specific drugs:

  • HLA-B*15:02 + carbamazepine: Strong association with SJS/TEN, particularly in Southeast Asian populations. Genetic screening is now required by the FDA.
  • HLA-B*58:01 + allopurinol: Strong association with SJS/TEN. Screening is recommended, especially in Southeast Asian and African American populations.
  • HLA-A*31:01 + carbamazepine: Associated with DRESS, particularly in European and Japanese populations.
If you are starting one of these medications and have not been offered genetic testing, ask about it. A simple blood test can dramatically reduce your risk.

Warning Signs—When to Go to the ER

If you have started a new medication in the past 1–6 weeks and develop any of the following, stop the medication and seek emergency care immediately:

  • Fever plus widespread rash (especially if painful or blistering)
  • Mouth sores, eye redness, or genital sores appearing with a new rash
  • Skin that peels or slides off with gentle pressure
  • Facial swelling plus rash plus fever (the DRESS triad)
  • Yellowing of the eyes or skin (liver involvement)
  • Dark urine, decreased urine output, or swollen ankles (kidney involvement)

Early drug withdrawal is the single most important intervention. The sooner the offending drug is stopped, the better the outcome.

After Recovery—What Happens Next

  • The offending drug is permanently contraindicated. It must be documented in every medical record, pharmacy profile, and on MedicAlert identification.
  • Related drugs in the same class may also need to be avoided (cross-reactivity varies by drug family).
  • Family members may benefit from genetic testing, especially for HLA-associated reactions.
  • Long-term follow-up is important: SJS/TEN survivors may develop chronic eye problems, scarring, or pigmentation changes. DRESS survivors need ongoing monitoring for autoimmune conditions that can emerge months later.
  • An allergist or dermatologist should be involved in the long-term care plan.

These reactions are rare—but they are not random. They follow patterns, they have genetic underpinnings, and they have warning signs. Knowing those signs, and acting on them early, is the difference between a close call and a catastrophe.
