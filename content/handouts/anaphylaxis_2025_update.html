ANAPHYLAXIS

A (Very) Short Tale of How “Protection” Became “Against-Protection”

Haden Allergy & Immunology • Patient Education Series

Picture the French Riviera in 1902. Two scientists—Charles Richet and Paul Portier (Richet won the Nobel Prize for discovering anaphylaxis)—are sailing aboard Princesse Alice, a yacht owned by the Prince of Monaco, which sounds like a nice way to spend a weekend if you can swing it. They are tinkering with a deadly jellyfish venom from Portuguese man-of-war tentacles.

Their goal? Inject tiny doses of the venom into dogs (I’m guessing these particular dogs were “unwilling volunteers”) to phylax (Greek for “protect”) them before a bigger exposure, much like an early idea of a vaccine. But that did not happen, and on the second shot the dogs collapsed instead of shaking it off. Protection had unexpectedly flipped to a worsened reaction. Stunned, the researchers coined a new term by splicing the Greek prefix “ana-” (against or reverse) onto “phylaxis”—and anaphylaxis was born. More than a century later, that same word still signals the immune system’s most dramatic, topsy-turvy malfunction: a defense response that backfires spectacularly and dangerously.

1. What Is Anaphylaxis?

Anaphylaxis is a severe—even life-threatening—allergic reaction. Picture an immune system that normally shields you from germs suddenly turning its fire hose on a harmless peanut, bee sting, or pill. Within minutes you may see a spreading rash or hives plus one or more of the following:

  • Swelling of lips, tongue, or throat
  • Trouble breathing or wheezing
  • Belly cramps, vomiting, or diarrhea
  • Dizziness, fainting, or a fast drop in blood pressure

Left untreated, anaphylaxis can spiral quickly—but early epinephrine turns the tide almost every time.

2. Why Does It Happen?

Your immune soldiers (antibodies, mast cells) mislabel a food, drug, or insect venom as dangerous. When they meet that trigger again, they release histamine and other chemicals that tighten airways, dilate blood vessels, and leak fluid—cue the hives and light-headedness.

  • Top culprits: peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, fish, milk, egg, sesame, wheat, soy, penicillin-family antibiotics, NSAIDs (aspirin, ibuprofen, etc.), and stinging insects.
  • Exercise-plus-food: some foods trigger a reaction only if you exercise within a few hours of eating them.
  • Idiopathic (medical for “We don’t know”): up to 20% of patients never find a clear trigger—your work-up rules out the usual suspects first.
Tip: Keep a diary of everything you ate, drank, swallowed, or slathered on skin in the four hours before symptoms. That detective work (plus skin or blood tests later, when the dust settles) often uncovers the cause.

3. What Does It Feel Like?

Along with hives, patients describe:

  • “Heat rushing to my face” or sudden flushing
  • Itchy palms or soles
  • “Lump in the throat” or hoarse voice
  • Thumping heart, “pins-and-needles,” a sense of doom
  • Crampy uterus (yes, that too)
  • Stomach or intestinal cramping, diarrhea

Some reactions skip the rash entirely—especially food-related throat swelling or wheeze. If you think to yourself, “My body feels wrong,” trust that instinct and treat: Epinephrine.

4. The One-Two-Three of Treatment

Step 1: Inject epinephrine. All injectable devices work through clothing. Aim for the mid-outer thigh and hold in place for three seconds (older EpiPens said ten—new technology is faster). Make sure friends and family know how to use your device. Come on, they’ve always wanted to stab you—give them their chance (at least to save you).

Step 2: Call 911. Even if you feel better after the shot, you need emergency observation.

Step 3: Second dose if needed. Keep two doses on hand. The second can be used if symptoms persist after 5–10 minutes.

5. After the Shot

  • Expect the jitters—pounding heart and shaky hands are normal and fade within 15–20 minutes.
  • ER observation: 6 hours minimum. About 15% of reactions rebound (biphasic anaphylaxis) several hours later. Make sure you can get any needed additional treatment.
  • Follow-up: You may be prescribed antihistamines, an oral steroid burst, or a rescue asthma inhaler if wheeze was prominent.

6. Living (Well) with Risk

  • Avoid known triggers—read labels, ask restaurants, wear insect-protective clothing outdoors.
  • Allergy shots (venom immunotherapy) virtually eliminate venom-sting anaphylaxis and reduce risk for some environmental allergies that exacerbate reactions.
  • Asthma control matters: well-controlled lungs weather anaphylaxis better.
  • MedicAlert ID speaks for you if you can’t. It should read “Anaphylaxis: Have epinephrine” so if you are “found down,” they know you have it and will look for it to give it to you.
  • Check the expiration date on every device twice a year—set phone reminders.
  • Practice with the trainer that comes in every box. Muscle memory trumps panic.

7. If a Reaction Strikes

Inject epinephrine—now. It is safe. It is the only thing that reliably stops progression of the reaction. Your own body makes epinephrine. It is an insurance policy you deserve.
  • Call 911.
  • Chew an antihistamine if you can swallow safely.
  • Ask the ER to draw a serum tryptase within 1–2 hours of onset; it confirms anaphylaxis and helps us fine-tune your plan.

And remember: you will almost certainly be fine when treated promptly. Epinephrine, EMS, and the ER are a safety net you deserve—never feel guilty using them. Better always to err on the side of caution. Stay prepared, stay calm, and keep living your life. Questions? Call our office anytime; peace of mind is part of the prescription.
