Hereditary Angioedema (HAE)

When Your Body’s “Swelling Switch” Gets Stuck On

Hereditary angioedema (HAE) is a genetic condition in which the body can suddenly swell—dramatically, painfully, and sometimes dangerously—without hives and without an allergic trigger. If that sounds like an allergy, you are not alone. Emergency rooms across the country treat HAE attacks with antihistamines, steroids, and epinephrine every day, and every day those treatments mostly fail, because HAE is not an allergy. It is a plumbing problem.

In most forms of HAE, the culprit is bradykinin, a molecule whose normal job is to make blood vessels slightly leaky during inflammation. In HAE, bradykinin can be produced too easily or cleared too slowly, so fluid escapes into tissues like water from a garden hose with a faulty shutoff valve. The swelling can hit your face, your hands, your gut, or—most dangerously—your throat. And because bradykinin does not care about antihistamines the way histamine does, the standard allergy playbook does not apply.

The good news is substantial. HAE is now one of the most treatable rare diseases in immunology. Modern on-demand medications can stop attacks in their tracks, and newer prevention options—including biologics and RNA-targeted therapies approved in 2025—can reduce attacks dramatically, often close to zero. The trick is getting the diagnosis right in the first place.

EMERGENCY: If you experience throat swelling, voice changes, difficulty swallowing, or trouble breathing—use your HAE rescue medication immediately and call 911.

The Big Clue: Swelling Without Hives

If you have ever described your episodes to a doctor and watched them reach for the antihistamine prescription pad, this section is for you. The hallmark of HAE is angioedema—deep tissue swelling—without the itchy, raised welts that define hives. Some patients feel tingling, tightness, or burning instead of itching. The swelling tends to last much longer than allergic swelling—often two to five days if untreated—and responds poorly to the medications that work beautifully for allergic reactions.

Common locations include the face, lips, eyelids, hands, feet, and genitals. Abdominal attacks deserve special mention: intestinal wall swelling can produce severe crampy pain, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea that convincingly mimics appendicitis, food poisoning, or a surgical abdomen. More than a few HAE patients have undergone unnecessary surgery before anyone checked a complement level.

The most dangerous location is the throat. Laryngeal swelling can cause hoarseness, difficulty swallowing, and airway obstruction. This is a genuine emergency and the reason every HAE patient needs a rescue plan.

A classic early warning: Some patients develop erythema marginatum—a non-itchy, map-like rash—hours before swelling begins. Think of it as a preview trailer. It is not hives, even though it can look vaguely similar at first glance.

Why This Happens: The Bradykinin Leak Pathway

Think of your blood vessels as garden hoses. Bradykinin is the faucet that makes them leaky on purpose—a useful feature during normal inflammation. Your body tightly controls this faucet through a protein called C1 inhibitor (C1-INH), which acts as a bouncer for several inflammatory systems simultaneously: the kallikrein-kinin system (where bradykinin is produced), the complement system, and parts of the coagulation cascade.

When C1-INH is missing, deficient, or dysfunctional, the bouncer leaves the door open. Kallikrein gets overactive, bradykinin rises, vessels leak, and tissues swell. The whole process has nothing to do with histamine, which is why antihistamines sit on the sidelines looking confused.

Types of HAE (Because Biology Loves Categories)

HAE Type I (most common): The body does not produce enough C1-INH protein. C1-INH levels are low, C1-INH function is low, and C4 is usually low even between attacks.

HAE Type II: The body produces normal or even elevated amounts of C1-INH, but the protein does not work properly. C1-INH levels look fine on paper, but functional testing reveals the defect. C4 is still usually low.

HAE with Normal C1-INH (formerly “Type III”): C1-INH level and function are both normal. The swelling is still often bradykinin-driven, but the genetic mechanism varies. Factor XII gene variants are a well-known cause. Other genes have been implicated, and some patients have no identifiable variant at all. Estrogen—from oral contraceptives or pregnancy—can influence certain subtypes.

Conditions That Can Look Similar

  • Acquired C1-INH deficiency: Usually later onset, sometimes associated with autoimmune or lymphoproliferative disorders. Not inherited.
  • ACE inhibitor angioedema: Bradykinin-driven swelling caused by a common blood pressure medication. Same mechanism, different cause. Your doctor should ask about this.
  • Allergic angioedema: Usually itchy, often accompanied by hives, faster onset, and—critically—it actually responds to allergy medications.

How HAE Presents in Real Life

Most patients begin having attacks in childhood or adolescence, with severity often increasing around puberty. If your family reunions include multiple people who swell unpredictably and have collectively accumulated enough emergency room visits to qualify for frequent flyer status, HAE should be on the radar.

Common Triggers

Triggers exist, but HAE is famously unpredictable. Sometimes the trigger is dental work, minor trauma, emotional stress, a viral illness, or a new medication. Sometimes it is just Tuesday.

  • Mechanical: Dental work, biting the cheek, tight shoes, repetitive hand use
  • Stress: Emotional or physical
  • Illness: Viral infections, particularly upper respiratory
  • Hormonal: Estrogen-containing contraceptives, pregnancy (effects vary by subtype)
  • Medications to avoid: ACE inhibitors can worsen bradykinin-mediated swelling and are generally contraindicated

Diagnosis: The Lab Trio That Matters

If HAE is suspected, diagnosis starts with blood tests—ideally drawn when you are well, because these markers are often abnormal even between attacks. The fact that your labs can be informative when you feel fine is one of the more cooperative things about this disease.

Core Screening Labs

  • C4 level: Often low in Type I and Type II HAE, even between attacks. This is the best screening test because it is cheap, widely available, and almost always abnormal.
  • C1-INH antigenic level: Measures how much C1-INH protein is present. Low in Type I, normal or high in Type II.
  • C1-INH functional assay: Measures whether the C1-INH protein actually works. Low in both Type I and Type II.

When Additional Testing Helps

  • C1q level: Helps distinguish hereditary from acquired forms. C1q is often low in acquired C1-INH deficiency but normal in hereditary forms.
  • Genetic testing: Not always required for Type I or II if labs are clear, but very helpful in HAE with normal C1-INH, complex family counseling, and unusual presentations.

A practical note: if you have recurrent swelling episodes without hives, especially with abdominal attacks or poor response to allergy medications, it is worth pursuing this workup even if you have been told it is stress, allergies, or idiopathic. Sometimes the most useful diagnostic test is the one nobody thought to order.

Why Allergy Medications Often Do Not Work

In allergic swelling, histamine is the main villain, and antihistamines are the hero. In HAE, bradykinin is usually the villain, and antihistamines are standing in the wrong movie theater. Steroids do not reliably help either. Epinephrine—the cornerstone of anaphylaxis treatment—often fails to stop HAE swelling.

This is clinically important but also practically important: if you have throat symptoms and you are not sure whether the swelling is allergic or bradykinin-mediated, treat it as an emergency regardless. Airway swelling is not a moment for philosophical certainty. Use whatever rescue medication you have and get to an emergency department.

Management: Two Goals

Modern HAE management has two objectives: stop attacks fast when they happen (on-demand therapy) and prevent attacks from happening in the first place (prophylaxis). Most patients benefit from having both strategies available. Every patient should have a rescue plan, and many benefit enormously from prevention.

On-Demand (Rescue) Treatment

The best time to treat an HAE attack is early—when you first notice tingling, tightness, or the earliest hint of swelling. Waiting until your ankle has become a cantaloupe is waiting too long.

  • C1-INH replacement: Plasma-derived C1-INH given intravenously for acute attacks. Replaces what is missing or dysfunctional.
  • Icatibant: A bradykinin B2 receptor antagonist given as a subcutaneous injection. Blocks bradykinin at its receptor.
  • Ecallantide: A kallikrein inhibitor given subcutaneously in monitored settings.
  • Sebetralstat (Ekterly): An oral on-demand treatment for acute HAE attacks, approved for ages 12 and older (FDA approved July 2025). This is a major convenience shift—patients can now carry a rapid-acting rescue option in a pill bottle instead of an injection kit.

Airway attacks: If swelling involves the tongue, throat, or voice box—use your on-demand medication immediately, call emergency services, and go to the emergency department. Airway swelling can progress unpredictably.

Prophylaxis (Prevention)

Prevention is considered when attacks are frequent, severe, affecting quality of life, or particularly dangerous—especially in patients with a history of laryngeal attacks.

  • Lanadelumab (Takhzyro): An injectable monoclonal antibody that inhibits plasma kallikrein. Widely used for prevention.
  • Garadacimab (Andembry): An injectable monoclonal antibody targeting Factor XIIa, upstream in the attack pathway. Once-monthly dosing. FDA approved June 2025 for ages 12 and older.
  • Donidalorsen (Dawnzera): An RNA-targeted therapy that reduces prekallikrein production—essentially turning down the volume on bradykinin’s precursor. Subcutaneous injection every four to eight weeks. FDA approved August 2025.
  • Berotralstat (Orladeyo): Once-daily oral prophylaxis. A kallikrein inhibitor in pill form.
  • C1-INH replacement for prevention: Subcutaneous plasma-derived C1-INH given on a regular schedule.
  • Older options: Attenuated androgens (such as danazol) are effective but carry significant side effects and long-term risks. Antifibrinolytics like tranexamic acid are sometimes used but are generally less effective than targeted modern therapies.

Short-Term Prophylaxis

Before high-risk procedures—dental extractions, intubation, major surgery—your HAE team may recommend short-term prophylaxis, typically C1-INH before the procedure, with on-demand medications immediately available. Think of it as pre-gaming, but medically responsible.

The “My Attack Starts in 20 Minutes” Action Plan

Create a written plan with your clinician, but the practical shape usually looks like this:

  1. Recognize early symptoms: tingling, tightness, early swelling, abdominal cramping, voice change
  2. Treat early with your on-demand medication. Do not wait and see if your pattern is one that escalates.
  3. Airway symptoms equal emergency: treat, call EMS, go to the emergency department.
  4. Do not rely on antihistamines or steroids as your primary plan. They may be used in mixed or uncertain cases, but HAE needs bradykinin-pathway therapy.

Special Situations

Pregnancy and Contraception

Estrogen can worsen attacks in some patients, particularly certain normal-C1-INH subtypes. The effects of pregnancy vary—some patients improve, some worsen, and some follow no discernible pattern whatsoever. Treatment planning during pregnancy is individualized; C1-INH therapies have a longer track record in pregnancy than most newer agents.

Children and Adolescents

HAE often begins early, and early diagnosis prevents unnecessary surgeries and emergency room visits. Several therapies now carry pediatric approvals, and the armamentarium has expanded considerably in recent years. If a child has recurrent unexplained swelling or abdominal pain, HAE belongs on the differential.

Travel

Carry at least two on-demand doses when possible. Bring a letter explaining HAE and your medications for airport security and international travel. Know where the nearest emergency care is at your destination. HAE does not take vacations, even when you do.

What to Tell Friends, Family, and Coworkers

A helpful one-liner: “This swelling is not an allergy. It is a genetic bradykinin problem. I have specific rescue medication for attacks, and throat swelling is an emergency.”

If you have a child with HAE, their school should know what symptoms look like, where rescue medications are stored, and when to call parents versus emergency medical services.

When to Suspect HAE in Someone Who Has Never Been Diagnosed

Consider evaluation if there is:

  • Recurrent angioedema without hives
  • Abdominal pain attacks with vomiting or diarrhea and normal imaging between episodes
  • Family history of similar swelling
  • Poor response to antihistamines or steroids
  • Swelling after dental work or minor trauma
  • Unexplained throat swelling

The average HAE patient waits over a decade for a correct diagnosis. That number is improving, but it remains too high. A complement level costs less than most of the imaging studies ordered during those years of diagnostic wandering.

The Bottom Line

HAE is serious—but it is also one of the most satisfying conditions to treat in immunology, because the tools now exist to stop attacks quickly and prevent them effectively. The newest prevention options include Factor XIIa inhibition, RNA-targeted prekallikrein reduction, and established biologic and oral preventive therapies. Collectively, they represent one of the most dramatic therapeutic expansions in any rare disease over the past decade.

If you or a family member has suspected HAE, the right diagnosis opens the door to the right medications—and to a life with fewer emergencies and more normal days. Start with a C4 level. It is a blood test, it is cheap, and it might change everything.
